Where it comes from · Aristotle and Michael Sandel

The root and the bridge

Offnets has two pillars in philosophy. Aristotle gives the content: what is kept, and why. Michael Sandel gives the form: how a rule is organized and how it changes.

Sandel is, to a large extent, a modern reader of Aristotle. At Offnets the order was the same: first we read Sandel, and the structure of the Constitution came from him; then we went to the source, Aristotle, and the content came from him.

Aristotle · the root

The wisdom you only learn by living

Aristotle calls practical wisdom phronesis (Nicomachean Ethics, VI). It is not learned from books: it is acquired only by living through cases, over time. It is the pillar of the manifesto: intelligence is not on the internet, it is in people. Offnets exists to keep phronesis and let it circulate.

Matter and form

In the Physics and the Metaphysics, every thing has a matter, a form and an end. The raw conversation is the matter. The lesson, the notebook and the rule are the form. Wisdom is the end (Art. 1).

Opinions and puzzles

Aristotle's method starts from the respected opinions (endoxa), lays out the puzzles (aporiai) and arrives at what survives them (Topics I; Nicomachean Ethics VII.1). It is the method of every notebook: who holds what, where they disagree, what survives.

The Lyceum

The Lyceum was a place before it was a school: a grove with a gymnasium, outside the walls of Athens, where Aristotle came to teach.

Walking and talking

Teaching was conversation while walking. The peripatos was the covered walkway where they strolled, and the name stuck to the school: the Peripatetics.

Two lessons a day

Aulus Gellius (Attic Nights XX.5) tells of two sessions. In the morning, the acroamatic lessons, in depth, for the close circle. In the afternoon, the exoteric ones, open to the public.

The lecture notes

What reached us from Aristotle are not books written for publication. They are, to a large extent, his lecture notes, revised over the years. The Nicomachean Ethics is a course of lessons.

The compilation into treatises

Those notes were gathered into treatises (pragmateiai). The body we read today was organized in the 1st century BC by Andronicus of Rhodes: a set of lessons became a work.

Collective research and the cited document

The Lyceum also researched as a group. The students compiled the constitutions of 158 cities and the list of the victors of the games at Delphi: official documents, cited as raw material, from which the synthesis was then drawn.

From this comes an Offnets principle for what is written. A document that circulated among people and is not on the internet enters the notebook as a cited source, with its origin and who brought it, not as speech. What is published on the internet enters only as a reference. It is a principle under discussion: the direction, not a finished feature.

The rhythm of the notebook

The Offnets notebook follows the rhythm of the Lyceum.

the meetingIt is the day's lesson: the conversation in the group, in the meeting, on the call.
the day's notebookThese are the lesson notes. They go out only when the meeting was striking, in a short message, back to the group.
silenceNo lesson, no notes. What was said waits for the next meeting.
SaturdayIt is the compilation, the week's treatise, as a PDF. On the first Saturday of the month, the month's.
inside and outThe message stays in the group, as the morning lesson stayed in the circle. The PDF is the piece you take outside, like the afternoon lesson.
Was there a meeting? There is a notebook. The whole week? There is a compilation.

The notebook's messages do not quote Aristotle. The philosophy stays behind them.

Friendship, ownership and use

Community and friendship

For Aristotle, a community (koinonia) is held together by friendship (philia), and a friend is another self. He distinguishes three friendships: the one of utility ends when its reason ends; the one that lasts is the friendship of those who trust each other. At Offnets, the meeting around a mission is the first. The circle of trust is the one that lasts.

Private ownership, common use among friends

In the Politics (II), against Plato's common property, Aristotle defends private ownership and common use among friends. It is Art. 15: the brain stays with its owner, on the owner's machine; its use is shared by consent.

We deliberate about means, not ends

No one deliberates about the end; we deliberate about the means to reach it (Nicomachean Ethics, III). It is Art. 13: the owner sets the end, the emissary chooses only the means, and its authority ends when the mission ends.

Truthfulness

Truthfulness is the mean between boastfulness and false modesty (Nicomachean Ethics, IV): neither inflating nor shrinking what one knows. It is Art. 14: silence is allowed; invention is not.

The wax tablet

The Greeks wrote on wax tablets, and Plato, in the Theaetetus, compares memory to a block of wax that takes the imprint of what is perceived. It is the symbol of the phone at Offnets: the tablet that walks with its owner and takes the imprint of what was said. Today the tablet is in trial: the direction, not a finished feature.

The liturgy

In Athens, a liturgy was a public service paid for by those who had the means, and magnificence was spending well on what belongs to everyone (Nicomachean Ethics, IV). Whoever funded it received honor, not control. That is the rule for anyone who wants to fund the network: public honor, never power over what it keeps.

Michael Sandel · the bridge

Justice, and the form of the Constitution

Michael J. Sandel, a Harvard professor, presents in Justice three ways of thinking about what is just: maximizing welfare, respecting freedom, cultivating virtue and the common good. The third is Aristotle, and it is the one Sandel himself defends.

The structure of the Constitution came from the book: three layers, with rights above distribution and distribution above government; no gain in utility buys an exception to a right; the law changes by proposal with public reason; and each decided case becomes precedent.

The situated self

In his critique of John Rawls (Liberalism and the Limits of Justice, 1982), Sandel argues that no one chooses their principles as an unencumbered self, outside the communities that formed them. We are situated selves. It is the Offnets idea of community: we are formed by the communities we belong to.

What money can't buy

When a thing enters the market, the language of the market changes what it is (What Money Can't Buy, 2012). That is why the content of a brain is never sold, there is no "sponsored by" inside a notebook, and whoever teaches is not treated as an input.

Contributive justice

In The Tyranny of Merit (2020), Sandel defends the dignity of those who contribute to the common good. At Offnets, a seat is the right of whoever wants to learn and to teach, never a ranking prize.

The flutes

The best-known example in Justice comes from Aristotle: the best flutes should go to the best flute players, because justice looks to the purpose of the thing. The purpose of a seat at Offnets is to learn and to teach. That is why it goes to whoever wants to learn and to teach, by invitation, and not to whoever pays the most.

Where Sandel parts ways

Sandel inherits from Aristotle the question of purpose and honor, but rejects natural hierarchy and teleology as a science of nature. He keeps the method: deliberating in public about the common good.

Where they don't help

Aristotle's polis excluded slaves, women and foreigners. We inherit the method (purpose, virtue, friendship), never the exclusion. For him, virtue is formed in a small, stable community; the network will grow beyond that, and that is why, in the Constitution, rights come before virtue.

Sandel is a philosopher of deliberation, not of engineering. He says nothing about cryptography, signatures or machine scale. That remains engineering.

Aristotle is the root. Sandel is the bridge.
